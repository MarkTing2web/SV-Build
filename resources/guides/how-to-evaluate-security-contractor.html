The Real Cost

1. Why Choosing the Wrong Contractor Costs More Than Paying More

The security contractor market in Singapore ranges from well-established licensed integrators who have been in operation for decades to one-man operations working without a licence, using grey-market equipment, and offering no meaningful after-sales support. The price difference between these two ends of the market is real. So is the consequence of getting it wrong.

A security system that was installed cheaply but incorrectly presents three categories of cost. The first is remedial work — fixing what was done wrong, which typically costs more than it would have cost to do it correctly the first time because some of the original work must be undone before the correct work can begin. The second is system incompatibility — equipment that cannot be integrated with what you already have, or cannot be expanded as your needs change, because the contractor specified a proprietary or end-of-life system. The third is absence of support — a contractor who is uncontactable, out of business, or unwilling to return when something fails two years after installation.

None of these costs appear in the original quotation. They appear later, when the system fails at an inconvenient moment, when you need to upgrade and discover the existing infrastructure cannot support it, or when you make an insurance claim and find that an unlicensed installation does not qualify for coverage.

The Correct Frame

The relevant comparison is not the price of Contractor A vs Contractor B for the same scope. It is the total cost of ownership over 10 years — including servicing, parts availability, and the likelihood that the contractor is still reachable when you need them. A system that costs S$1,500 less today and requires S$3,000 in remedial work in year two is not cheaper. It is more expensive, and it caused disruption in the process.

Legal Requirement

2. Licensing — What Is Required in Singapore and How to Verify

In Singapore, the installation of security systems — including CCTV, alarm systems, access control, and intercoms — requires a valid Police Security Industry Act (PSIA) licence. This is not optional. It is a legal requirement, and its absence has direct consequences for both the contractor and the client.

What the PSIA Licence Covers

The Police Security Industry Act (Cap 250A) regulates businesses engaged in the provision of security services and the installation of security equipment in Singapore. A licensed security contractor has undergone background screening, met minimum competency standards, and is subject to ongoing regulatory oversight by the Singapore Police Force.

The licence is issued per company — not per individual technician — and has an expiry date. A contractor whose licence has lapsed is operating illegally, regardless of how long they have been in business or how many installations they have completed. The licence number is typically displayed on the contractor's quotations, letterhead, and website. If it is not visible, ask for it directly.

How to Verify a PSIA Licence

The Singapore Police Force maintains a register of licensed security service providers. You can verify a contractor's licence number directly through the SPF website or by contacting the Licensing Division. When verifying, confirm three things: the licence number matches the company name on the quotation, the licence is currently valid (not expired), and the licence scope covers the type of installation being performed.

Why This Matters Beyond Compliance

Many Singapore home and commercial property insurance policies require that security systems be installed by a PSIA-licensed contractor as a condition of any claim relating to break-in, theft, or property damage. An installation by an unlicensed contractor may be technically functional but legally non-compliant — meaning your insurer can decline a claim on the grounds that the security system does not meet their installation standards. Verify the licence before the installation, not after you need to make a claim.

Document Analysis

3. Reading a Quotation — What Should Be There and What Should Not

A security quotation is a window into how a contractor thinks about the job. A well-structured quotation tells you the contractor has assessed your specific site. A vague or template-heavy quotation tells you they have not.

What a Good Quotation Contains

  • Line-item breakdown: Every piece of equipment listed separately with brand, model number, and quantity. Not "CCTV system — S$2,800" but "4 × Hikvision DS-2CD2T47G2 4MP camera, 1 × Hikvision DS-7604NI-K1/4P 4-channel NVR, 4 × 50m Cat 6 cable runs."
  • Scope of supply: A clear statement of what is included — equipment, installation labour, cabling, conduit, configuration, and testing.
  • Scope of exclusions: What is not included — civil works, electrical supply points, patching and painting after cable installation. Exclusions are not red flags; undisclosed exclusions are.
  • Warranty terms: Duration, what is covered, and who provides the warranty — the manufacturer, the contractor, or both.
  • Payment milestones: The standard Singapore practice is deposit on acceptance, progress payment on hardware delivery, balance on commissioning. Be cautious of requests for full payment upfront.
  • PSIA licence number: Should appear on the quotation header or footer.

How to Compare Quotations That Look Different

Two quotations for "a CCTV system" covering the same property can legitimately differ by S$2,000 or more and both be reasonable — if they specify different cameras, different NVR capacity, different cable run lengths, or different labour scope. They can also differ because one contractor has included items the other has excluded. You cannot compare the bottom-line number without first confirming the scope is equivalent.

The method is to build a comparison table: list every line item from the most detailed quotation, then check whether each item appears in the other quotations, and at what specification. Items present in one quote and absent from another are either exclusions (check the exclusion list) or omissions (ask the contractor directly). A contractor who cannot explain why their price is lower than a competitor's is telling you something.

What a Poor Quotation Looks Like

Warning Sign What It May Indicate
Lump-sum price with no line itemsCannot be compared with other quotes; scope is undefined; contractor may substitute equipment during installation
Brand or model not specified — just "IP camera" or "alarm system"Contractor is not committed to a specific specification; may substitute cheaper equipment after acceptance

Due Diligence

4. Questions to Ask Before You Sign

These questions are not adversarial. A competent, established contractor will answer all of them directly and without hesitation. The questions that receive evasive or incomplete answers are the ones that matter most.

Question What a Good Answer Looks Like What to Watch For
Can you provide your PSIA licence number for verification? Immediate — number provided without hesitation, matches company name Delay, evasion, or "we are applying for renewal" — check the register independently
Are the brands and models in your quote available new from authorised distributors in Singapore? Yes, with distributor name provided if asked Vague answers about "equivalent" or "similar" models — potential grey-market stock
Who will be doing the installation — your own technicians or subcontractors? Direct answer — own team or named subcontractor with their qualifications "Our team" used loosely to mean ad-hoc subcontractors with no accountability
What is your response time for a service call after installation? Specific — "next business day for non-urgent, same day for system-down" Vague promises with no commitment — "we will do our best"
What happens if a component fails within the warranty period? Clear process — who to contact, expected response time, whether replacement is on-site or depot Redirecting responsibility to the manufacturer — the contractor should be the primary point of contact
Can you provide references from similar installations you have completed? Yes, with contact details or at minimum property type and location No references available, or references that cannot be independently contacted
How long has your company been in operation? Clear answer with company registration verifiable on ACRA Newly registered company with claims of extensive experience — verify registration date
What documentation will I receive at handover? Installation certificate, as-built drawings, user credentials, programming backup, warranty cards "We will send it later" with no commitment — documentation is often not produced if not requested upfront
What are your annual maintenance terms after the warranty period? Clear pricing structure for annual servicing or ad-hoc call-out No maintenance offering — indicates a contractor focused on installation revenue, not long-term relationships
If I want to expand the system in three years, can it be done on the same platform? Yes, with specific explanation of how expansion works on the specified system Vague reassurance without specifics — check the manufacturer's documentation yourself
Brand and Specification Literacy

5. Understanding the Brands Being Specified

You do not need to be a security engineer to evaluate whether the brands in a quotation are appropriate. You need to know a small number of things — whether the brand is current, whether parts are available in Singapore, and whether the contractor has an authorised relationship with the manufacturer.

Is the Brand Still Supported?

Security hardware brands regularly discontinue product lines. Panasonic has discontinued several PABX and security camera ranges. Certain Hikvision and Dahua camera series have reached end-of-life firmware support. A contractor who specifies equipment from a discontinued range may be selling grey-market or old stock that cannot receive firmware security updates — a meaningful concern for any internet-connected camera or access control system.

The check is simple: search the brand's official website for the exact model number in the quotation. If it does not appear on the current product listing, ask the contractor directly whether the model is current or end-of-life, and what firmware support is available.

Grey Market vs Authorised Stock

Grey market equipment is hardware sold outside the manufacturer's authorised distribution channel — often imported from a different region where the model is cheaper, or sourced from overstocked inventory without the manufacturer's knowledge. Grey market equipment may be identical in function to authorised stock but it typically carries no manufacturer warranty in Singapore, may have firmware that cannot be updated through official channels, and is not supported by the local distributor for spare parts or technical assistance.

The practical indicator is price. If a quotation is significantly cheaper than others for the same brand and model, ask the contractor whether the equipment is sourced from an authorised Singapore distributor and whether a local manufacturer warranty applies. A contractor using authorised stock should be able to name the local distributor without hesitation.

Proprietary Systems and Vendor Lock-In

Some security systems use proprietary protocols that only work with the same brand's equipment throughout. The access readers only work with that brand's controller; the cameras only record to that brand's NVR; the alarm sensors only communicate with that brand's panel. This is not inherently wrong — proprietary integration can be more reliable than generic open-standard integration. But it means that all future expansions, upgrades, and servicing must go through the same vendor. If that vendor raises prices, becomes unresponsive, or exits the market, your options are limited.

Before accepting a proprietary system, ask: what happens if I want to add equipment from a different brand in three years? What happens if the vendor is no longer operating? What open-standard alternatives exist for this application?

Assessment Standard

6. What a Proper Site Assessment Looks Like

A site assessment is the foundation of a correct security system specification. A contractor who provides a quotation without visiting your property is specifying a system for a property they have never seen — which means they are guessing at camera positions, cable run lengths, lock compatibility, and the specific vulnerabilities that matter for your site.

What Should Happen During a Site Assessment

  • The engineer walks every part of the property — all entrances and exits, the perimeter, all floors, roof access points, and any outbuildings or car park areas
  • They ask about your specific concerns, how the property is used, how many people have access, and what your priorities are
  • They identify camera positions that achieve the coverage you need — not just the easiest positions to install
  • They check existing infrastructure — what cabling is already present, where conduit exists, where power is available
  • They assess the doors and frames to confirm lock compatibility before specifying lock types
  • They note any site-specific challenges — lighting conditions, exposure to weather, existing physical security, and any regulatory constraints for the property type
  • They provide a preliminary recommendation — not a binding quotation, but a clear indication of what they recommend and why

What a Minimal or Poor Site Assessment Looks Like

A poor site assessment is a brief walk of the main areas with a phone photo taken of each door, followed by a template quotation issued the same day. The engineer asks few questions, does not walk the full perimeter, and does not examine existing infrastructure. The quotation that follows has generic specifications that do not reflect the specific conditions of your property.

The test is simple: after the assessment, does the contractor's recommendation reflect specific details about your property — the condition of your gate post, the distance from the main gate to the house, the door frame material on your side entrance, the angle of the sun on your driveway at the time of day when most deliveries arrive? If the recommendation could have been written for any property without a site visit, the site assessment was inadequate.

Warning Signals

7. Red Flags in a Security Proposal

Most problems with a security contractor become visible before the installation begins, if you know what to look for. These are the specific signals that warrant either a direct question or a decision to proceed with a different contractor.

Red Flag Why It Matters
No PSIA licence number on the quotation or website May indicate unlicensed operation. Verify independently before proceeding — do not rely on verbal assurance.
Quotation issued within hours of a site visit Suggests a template quotation rather than a site-specific specification. A thorough assessment takes time to document.
Unusually low price with no explanation Almost always attributable to grey-market equipment, excluded items, or substandard labour. Ask specifically which it is.
Pressure to sign quickly — "price valid for 48 hours only" Legitimate contractors do not use artificial urgency. This tactic is designed to prevent you from comparing quotes properly.
Request for full payment upfront The standard in Singapore is a deposit structure. Full upfront payment removes the contractor's financial incentive to complete the job to standard.
No physical office address or verifiable company registration Verify the company on ACRA. A company that has no verifiable existence cannot be held accountable after installation.
Dismissiveness about documentation at handover "You don't need as-built drawings for a residential job" is incorrect. Documentation protects you — not the contractor. Resistance to providing it is a signal.
No mention of warranty terms or after-sales support A contractor who does not raise this topic proactively is unlikely to prioritise it reactively when something fails.
Specification of end-of-life or discontinued models Indicates the contractor is clearing old stock or working with grey-market inventory. No firmware support, limited parts availability.
Unable to name the local distributor for the brands they specify An authorised installer of any major brand knows their local distributor. Inability to name them suggests an unauthorised supply chain.
A Note on Price

Price alone is not a red flag. A significantly lower price accompanied by a clear explanation — smaller company with lower overheads, different specification that genuinely costs less, a promotion on specific equipment — can be entirely legitimate. The red flag is a significantly lower price with no explanation, combined with resistance to questions about why. Price and transparency together tell the full story.

Long-Term Assurance

8. Warranties, After-Sales Support, and Maintenance

The installation is the beginning of the relationship between a property owner and their security contractor — not the end of it. A security system requires maintenance, occasional repair, and eventual upgrade. The contractor's approach to after-sales support determines the real cost of the system over its operating life.

What "One Year Warranty" Actually Means

Almost every security contractor in Singapore offers a one-year warranty. What this means in practice varies significantly. At minimum, a one-year warranty should cover: defects in the equipment supplied, defects in the installation workmanship, and return visits to rectify both at no charge to the client within the warranty period.

What a warranty does not typically cover: accidental damage, damage caused by power surges or flooding, consumables such as hard drives in NVRs (which have a finite write cycle), and equipment failure caused by the client's own network or power infrastructure. These exclusions are reasonable — but they should be stated explicitly in the warranty terms, not discovered when you make a claim.

Equipment Warranty vs Workmanship Warranty

These are two separate things. Equipment warranty covers hardware failure — a camera that stops working, a lock that fails to release. This warranty typically runs from the manufacturer through the contractor to the client. Workmanship warranty covers installation defects — a cable that was not properly terminated and fails at the connector, a camera that was not properly weatherproofed and allows moisture ingress. This warranty runs from the contractor to the client. Both should be specified and both should have clear duration and coverage terms.

Annual Maintenance — What Is Reasonable to Expect

After the warranty period, most security contractors offer annual maintenance contracts or ad-hoc call-out service. Annual maintenance typically covers one scheduled visit per year for system inspection, cleaning, firmware updates, battery checks, and a full function test of all components. It should also cover a defined number of reactive call-outs per year for faults that arise between scheduled visits.

The questions to ask before signing a maintenance contract: what is included in the annual visit and what is excluded, what is the call-out response time for different fault categories, and what is the labour rate for work that falls outside the maintenance scope. A contractor who cannot provide clear answers to these questions before the contract is signed will not provide better answers after it is signed.

Project Completion

9. The Handover — What You Should Receive When the Job Is Done

The handover is the formal completion of the installation. What you receive at handover determines whether you can manage, service, and if necessary change your security system in the future — or whether you are permanently dependent on the original contractor for access to your own system.

The Complete Handover Checklist

Document / Item What It Contains Why You Need It
Installation certificate Signed document confirming the system was installed by a PSIA-licensed contractor, with licence number and date Required for insurance claims; confirms legal compliance of the installation
As-built drawings Floor plan showing every camera position, sensor location, cable route, and equipment location as actually installed Enables any future contractor to understand and work on your system; essential for fault-finding
Admin credentials Username and password for all management software, NVR, access control software, and network equipment Without these you cannot manage your own system or engage a different contractor for servicing
Programming backup Configuration file or documented settings for the alarm panel, access control system, and NVR Allows the system to be restored to its correct configuration after a hardware failure
Warranty documentation Manufacturer warranty cards or registration confirmation for all major hardware components Required to make warranty claims directly with the manufacturer if the contractor is unavailable
User training Demonstration of how to operate the system — arming and disarming, adding users, reviewing footage, handling common faults Ensures you can operate the system you have paid for without calling the contractor for routine tasks
Cable schedule / label reference List of all cable labels and what each cable connects Enables fault isolation and future expansion without re-tracing every cable from scratch
Key Point

The admin credentials for your security system belong to you — not to the contractor. A contractor who withholds admin credentials, or who configures a system so that only they can access the admin interface, is creating a dependency that serves their interests, not yours. Require all admin credentials at handover as a condition of final payment. This is a standard expectation and any legitimate contractor will comply without objection.

The Decision

10. Making the Final Decision

By the time you have worked through the previous nine sections, you should have enough information to make a well-founded decision. This section sets out the framework for making it.

The Evaluation Framework

Rather than choosing on price, evaluate contractors across five dimensions. Price is one of them — and it matters — but it carries less weight than the others when the difference between a good and a poor outcome is measured in years of system performance, not dollars on a quotation.

Dimension What to Look For
Compliance PSIA licence current and verifiable. Company registered on ACRA. No unresolved regulatory issues.
Specification quality Line-item quotation with current brand models from authorised distributors. Scope clearly defined. Exclusions stated.
Assessment rigour Site visit was thorough. Recommendation reflects your specific property. Engineer asked the right questions.
After-sales commitment Clear warranty terms. Maintenance offering available. Response time commitments stated. Documentation committed at handover.
Price Competitive within the market for equivalent scope and specification. Significantly lower prices explained and justified.

When to Walk Away

Walk away if the contractor cannot provide a verifiable PSIA licence number. Walk away if they cannot name the local distributor for the brands they are specifying. Walk away if they resist providing admin credentials at handover. Walk away if they request full payment before installation begins. Walk away if the site assessment consisted of a brief walkthrough and a template quotation issued within the hour.

None of these are unreasonable standards. Every legitimate, established security contractor in Singapore meets all of them as a matter of course.

The Bottom Line

Choosing a security contractor is not primarily a price decision. It is a decision about who will be responsible for the infrastructure that protects your property, your family, or your business for the next 10–15 years. The questions in this guide are not difficult to ask, and the answers are not difficult to evaluate. A contractor who answers them clearly, produces documentation without being asked twice, and prices their work transparently is telling you something important about how they will behave after the installation is complete — which is when the relationship actually begins.
