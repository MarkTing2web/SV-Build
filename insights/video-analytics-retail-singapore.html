Video Analytics in Singapore Retail: People Counting, Heatmaps and Operational Intelligence

Modern IP cameras do more than record. Built-in analytics turn surveillance footage into operational data — footfall counts, zone heatmaps, and staffing patterns that used to require manual observation or dedicated sensor hardware.

What Video Analytics Actually Measures

Video analytics processes camera footage on the camera itself — using an onboard processor called an NPU (Neural Processing Unit) — rather than sending raw video to a server for analysis. This edge processing approach means the analytics run continuously without requiring additional server infrastructure or network bandwidth for the computation.

For retail applications, the three most useful outputs are:

  • People counting — the number of individuals who cross a defined line or enter a defined zone during a time period. Typically deployed at store entrances to measure footfall. When calibrated correctly on a well-positioned camera, modern people counting algorithms achieve accuracy within ±5% under normal conditions — sufficient for operational decision-making.
  • Zone heatmaps — a visualisation of where people spend time within the store. The camera tracks movement across the floor plan over an accumulation period and generates a map showing high-dwell zones versus pass-through corridors versus areas that are consistently avoided. This is different from a simple traffic count — it shows where attention lands, not just where feet go.
  • Occupancy and crowd density — the number of people present in a defined area at any given moment. Used for queue management, compliance with occupancy limits, and identifying peak congestion points.

People Counting: What It Tells You

A door counter answers the question: how many people came in? That number, tracked over time, becomes the basis for several operational decisions.

Conversion rate benchmarking. If you know how many people entered the store and how many transactions were completed, you have a conversion rate. Without an accurate footfall count, conversion rate is unmeasurable — you are left with transaction data alone, which tells you what sold but not how many people walked past without buying.

Campaign effectiveness. A promotion, a window display change, or a marketing push is visible in the footfall data. Did the number of people who entered increase during the campaign period? Did it increase at the right times? Comparing footfall before, during, and after a campaign gives a measurable signal that complements sales data.

Peak hour mapping. When are the busiest hours, by day of week? The data answers this over weeks and months, with enough resolution to identify patterns that casual observation misses — for example, that Saturday afternoons are consistently busier than Saturday mornings, or that Friday evenings have high footfall but low conversion.

Worth Noting

People counting shows the top of the funnel — how many people entered. What happens inside the store — whether staff were available, whether the product was in stock, whether the layout supported the browsing journey — requires additional context. The analytics surface the question; investigation provides the answer.

Zone Heatmaps: Where Attention Goes

A heatmap accumulated over a sufficient period reveals the actual traffic pattern inside a store — often different from what the layout was designed to produce.

Hot zones are areas where people consistently stop and dwell. These are high-value positions for featured products, promotions, and displays. A hot zone near the entrance captures attention early. A hot zone near the back of the store indicates a product or category that draws deliberate shoppers.

Cold zones are areas that footfall consistently bypasses. This may reflect poor visibility from the entrance, an uninviting category presentation, or a layout that does not direct traffic naturally. A persistently cold zone containing high-margin products is a layout problem worth addressing.

Traffic corridors are paths people use to move through the store without stopping. Understanding the natural flow helps position impulse or cross-sell products where they will be seen by the most people in transit.

For chain retailers operating multiple outlets, heatmap comparison across locations reveals whether certain layout configurations consistently outperform others — separating the effect of layout from the effect of location or foot traffic volume.

Manpower Planning: Matching Staff to Footfall

One of the more practical applications of people counting and occupancy data is staffing alignment. The question is straightforward: does the number of staff on the floor match the number of customers present at any given time?

Without footfall data, staffing decisions are based on experience and historical transaction patterns. With footfall data, the analysis becomes more precise. If the busiest period on a Wednesday is consistently between 12:30pm and 1:30pm but staffing rosters were built around a 1pm–2pm assumption, the mismatch is visible in the data.

For chain retailers, this analysis is most useful at scale. A single store's data is informative. Across 10 or 20 outlets, patterns emerge that reveal systemic under-staffing or over-staffing at particular times or in particular formats. Adjusting rosters based on actual footfall patterns — rather than estimates — is a direct operational efficiency gain.

This is a question of aggregate floor coverage, not individual employee monitoring. The analytics measure customer presence and density. The operational question being answered is whether the store had sufficient staff to serve the customers who were there — matched against the actual traffic pattern the data reveals.

Campaign Measurement in Practice

The most direct application of footfall analytics for retail marketing is measuring whether a campaign actually drove people into the store. A standard measurement approach:

  1. Establish a baseline — measure footfall for two to four weeks before the campaign. Account for day-of-week variation by comparing like-for-like days.
  2. Run the campaign — with footfall counting active throughout.
  3. Compare — did footfall increase during the campaign period? By how much, and at which times? Did the increase correlate with specific campaign channels or dates?
  4. Adjust — use the data to refine future campaigns. If one channel drove footfall and another had no measurable effect, that is actionable information for the next cycle.

This measurement approach is straightforward to implement once a people counting system is in place. The analytics platform records timestamped counts continuously — the analysis is a reporting exercise, not an additional technical deployment.

For multi-outlet chains, campaign measurement across locations also reveals whether the campaign performed consistently or whether certain outlets responded significantly better than others — which may reflect local factors worth investigating.

What the Technology Does Not Do Well

Video analytics is frequently over-specified and over-sold. Being clear about limitations is more useful than claiming capability the system cannot reliably deliver.

Accuracy degrades in challenging conditions. People counting accuracy drops when there is significant occlusion — people walking in groups, closely spaced, or partially obscured. Camera positioning and mounting height matter significantly. A poorly positioned camera will produce counts that are directionally useful but not precise enough for detailed analysis.

Heatmaps require accumulation time. A heatmap based on one day's data is noisy. Meaningful patterns emerge over weeks. Interpreting a heatmap from insufficient data leads to incorrect conclusions about zone performance.

Analytics cannot explain behaviour. The data shows what happened — where people went, how long they stayed, when they came. It does not show why. Combining analytics data with staff observation and customer feedback gives a fuller picture than analytics alone.

The camera must support it. Not all cameras include analytics capability. A network of standard recording cameras cannot be upgraded to a heatmap system without replacing the cameras — the analytics run on the camera's own onboard processor, which standard recording cameras do not have. For retailers planning a CCTV refresh, specifying analytics-capable cameras from the outset adds minimal cost to the hardware and enables the full capability without any additional infrastructure.

Securevision's View

The value of video analytics for retail is not in the technology — it is in having consistent, comparable data over time. A single footfall reading tells you little. Six months of footfall data across multiple outlets, correlated with sales, promotions, and roster changes, tells you quite a lot. The investment case is strongest for retailers who will actually use the data, not those who want the capability in principle.

Is This Right for Your Retail Operation?

People counting and heatmap analytics make most sense for retailers who:

  • Operate multiple outlets and want comparable data across locations
  • Run regular promotions and want to measure their effect on footfall
  • Are making layout or fixture changes and want to assess the impact
  • Have peak-hour staffing challenges and want data to support roster decisions

A single-outlet independent retailer can benefit from people counting, but the return on investment is clearer at scale — where the same analytical framework is applied across many stores and the insights compound.

For retailers already planning a CCTV upgrade or new installation, specifying cameras with built-in analytics capability adds minimal cost to the camera hardware and enables the analytics without any additional infrastructure.
